What the models could do with it

Trained on 2005–2019, tuned on 2020–2022, scored once on 2023–2025. Split by year rather than at random, because a cell resembles itself from one year to the next and a random split would report an accuracy no real season would repeat.

Where goes wrong

What the forest leans on

Weather in orange, fire history in slate. The strongest single predictor is not fire history at all.

Why that follows

A wet growing season builds the grass that will carry a fire months later, and how thirsty the air has been decides how early that grass cures enough to burn. Vapour pressure deficit and wet-season rainfall outrank every record of where fire has been before.

On fire history alone the whole field was flat: a single decision tree matched the LSTM to three decimal places. The sequence model only earns its place once it can also see the wet season.

How it is kept honest

Rule one

Nothing sees the year it predicts

Fire-history features come from the same table as the label, so an off-by-one would let a model read the answer. Two tests check it: sampled features are recomputed from the raw record, and a year’s labels are scrambled to confirm not one of its features moves.

Rule two

Weather stops at 30 April

The fire season runs May to October. August weather would describe the fire rather than anticipate it. Everything the model uses is known by the end of the wet season, so it could genuinely be run in May.

Stated limit

The month is approximate

NAFI assigns each burnt pixel the month covering most of the interval in which it was detected. Close to the July boundary that attribution is imprecise, which is a real limit on the early/late split rather than one worth hiding.
